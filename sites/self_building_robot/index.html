A plan, 2026

The robot that
builds itself.

Two arms. Two eyes. No legs. Its only job is to put together another one of itself, and the parts and printers that make it.

The bootstrap

If a person with the gripper can build it, the robot can.

Start with no automation at all. People build the whole robot, gripper included, and pack it, holding one copy of the robot's gripper in each hand. Nothing else. Every build is also training data, filmed from cameras at eye height.

The one design that matters

Needle-nose pliers, not a hand.

Small enough to stay out of a person's way, simple enough to make by the million. One joint, used in pairs. The tips do the extra work: pads to pinch, a notch for screws, a pocket for a screwdriver bit, a cutter for wire. The robot's wrist turns, so it can drive a screw.

Eyes at eye level

Two cameras, spaced like a person's eyes, at the height a person sees from. What people record is what the robot will see.

Feels through its motors

No force sensor. When the jaws touch the part, the motor works harder, and its current says how hard it is squeezing.

Stage 2

Then it builds its sibling.

The robot repeats the steps people taught it: arms onto a torso, a head onto a neck, screws into holes. It must also build a printer kit nobody designed for it, so it can't cheat by making its own parts too easy.

Printers that build printers

The factory grows itself.

Presses and molds need machines robots can't build. Printers are mostly printed parts. So the robots tend a printer farm, and build more printers from what it prints. One becomes two becomes four.

Closing the loop

Push the start line down.

Today the robots start from bought motors, screws and electronics plus printed parts. Each step down removes one thing from the shopping list, until they start at the mine. A pliers gripper won't dig; the robots build the excavators that do. Chips are always bought.

    Materials

    Mostly iron. No rare earths.

    Steel structure and iron cores. Aluminium wire. Plant-based plastic. Big joints use switched reluctance motors, just steel and coils. The gripper gets ferrite magnets, made from iron oxide, arranged in a Halbach ring to make the most of them.

    How it ships

    One robot and a box of parts.

    The customer gets a built robot and a kit. The first thing it does at home is build the second one.

    Sizes

    Bigger or smaller, one step at a time.

    A large robot can't build a tiny one in one go. Large builds medium, medium builds small, small builds tiny, about half the size each time. The same design stretches from a quarter of human size to a few times bigger before it has to change.

    The penny that doubles

    A penny doubled every day for 30 days is $5.4 million.

    Robots that build robots grow the same way. One robot, doubling 33 times, is 8.6 billion.
